# POV-AI-Storytelling

[中文](README_CN.md) | English

> A practical framework for creating first-person AI visual stories.

**POV-AI-Storytelling** is an open storytelling framework for turning a simple idea into a serialized AI-generated visual narrative.

It is not just a prompt collection.

It focuses on the entire creative process:

**Idea → Hidden Truth → Story Structure → Suspense → Visual Evidence → Shot Design → AI Generation → Continuity → Editing → Release**

Chinese name:

**第一视角 AI 视觉叙事方法论**

---

## What is this?

AI image and video tools make it increasingly easy to create impressive visuals.

But impressive visuals alone do not make a story.

A strange building, a monster, an abandoned laboratory, or a surreal world may look interesting for a few seconds. What keeps people watching is something else:

> **They want to know what happens next.**

This project explores a practical method for building serialized visual stories using AI-generated images and video.

The core idea is simple:

> Start with an ordinary and believable reality.  
> Introduce one small abnormality.  
> Let the abnormality escalate step by step.  
> Leave visual evidence instead of explaining everything.  
> Reveal the truth only when the audience has enough pieces to understand it.

The goal is to make the audience feel that they are not simply looking at AI images.

They are **experiencing an event**.

---

## Core Philosophy

A good AI visual story is not:

> “30 beautiful AI images.”

It is:

> **30 images that make the audience experience one continuous event.**

The most important skill is therefore not prompt writing.

It is **information control**.

The creator decides:

- What the audience sees
- What the audience does not see
- When a clue appears
- How much of the clue is visible
- When an abnormality escalates
- When a question is answered
- When a larger question should appear

AI is the camera.

The creator is the director.

## The Basic Formula

```text
Ordinary Reality
+
One Abnormal Event
=
Story Hook
```

Examples:

```text
A student preparing for an entrance exam
+
A table suddenly appears in an empty field
```

```text
An office worker joins a lucid-dream experiment
+
His colleague claims he experienced hundreds of years overnight
```

```text
A university student visits his grandmother
+
She gives him a jar containing an unknown black liquid
```

```text
A worker is assigned to guard a grass field
+
Six strange towers must be fed meat every day
```

The world does not need to be complicated at the beginning.

It only needs one question that makes the audience want an answer.

13. Reveal

## 1. Start With a Question

Before building the world, ask:

> **What is the one question the audience desperately wants answered?**

Examples:

- Why is there a table in the middle of nowhere?
- Who is actually messaging the protagonist?
- What is underneath the grass field?
- Why does Floor -37 contain an endless grassland?
- What happened inside the dream experiment?

A strong story can usually be summarized as one clear mystery.

If the concept takes several paragraphs to explain before it becomes interesting, it is probably still too complicated.

---

## 2. Know the Truth Before the Audience Does

The audience does not need to know the answer.

The creator usually should.

Write down:

```text
What actually happened?

Who caused it?

Why did it happen?

What does the protagonist misunderstand?

What does the audience misunderstand?

What will eventually reveal the truth?
```

Then work backward.

Instead of writing:

```text
Strange Event
→ Stranger Event
→ Even Stranger Event
→ Random Twist
```

Try:

```text
Final Truth
↓
What evidence would this truth leave behind?
↓
Which evidence can appear early?
↓
Which clues can be misunderstood?
↓
Which clue finally changes the meaning of everything?
```

This turns random weirdness into foreshadowing.

---

## 3. Build an Ordinary Reality First

The abnormal only feels abnormal when normality has already been established.

Before the mystery becomes important, show ordinary life.

For example:

- Going to class
- Riding a bicycle
- Returning to an apartment
- Eating dinner
- Studying
- Working
- Talking with a roommate
- Checking a phone
- Organizing documents

These scenes are not filler.

They establish the rules of reality.

> **The more believable the normal world is, the stronger the abnormality becomes.**

---

## 4. Escalate the Abnormality

Do not reveal the strongest image too early.

A useful progression is:

```text
Level 1 — Strange, but ignorable
Level 2 — It appears again
Level 3 — Something has changed
Level 4 — It seems intentional
Level 5 — It becomes personal
Level 6 — Hard evidence appears
Level 7 — The audience's interpretation changes
```

Each new event should increase at least one of these:

- danger
- specificity
- personal relevance
- impossibility
- emotional weight
- amount of evidence

Do not repeat the same strange event five times.

**Escalate it.**

---

## 5. One Image, One Main Narrative Job

A common mistake is putting every important clue into one image.

Instead of:

```text
Enter room
+
see mannequin
+
see photos
+
see bed
+
see evidence
+
see killer
```

break it apart:

```text
Shot 1 — Enter the room
Shot 2 — Notice the mannequin
Shot 3 — Approach the mannequin
Shot 4 — Recognize a familiar object
Shot 5 — Examine the table
Shot 6 — Notice something under the blanket
Shot 7 — Lift the blanket
Shot 8 — Discover the photographs
```

The audience should feel like they are exploring.

Not reading an illustration.

A simple test:

> **If this image is removed, does the story lose information, emotion, or tension?**

If not, remove it.

---

## 6. Answer One Question, Create Another

A strong visual sequence often follows this pattern:

```text
Question
↓
Partial Answer
↓
New Question
↓
Partial Answer
↓
Larger Question
```

This creates forward momentum.

The audience keeps receiving answers without ever feeling completely satisfied.

---

## 7. Use Visual Evidence

Whenever possible, **show evidence instead of explaining information**.

Useful evidence can include:

- photographs
- clothing
- tools
- maps
- keys
- receipts
- journals
- research notes
- medical records
- surveillance footage
- chat history
- hard drives
- work badges
- vehicles
- newspapers
- old buildings
- personal belongings

Objects should not only decorate the scene.

They should tell the story.

---

## 8. Control How Much the Audience Can See

Suspense is not simply hiding information.

It is controlling **how much information is visible at each moment**.

Think in percentages:

```text
10% — only the corner of a photograph
20% — an unclear object in the distance
40% — several photos of the same location
60% — evidence connecting the location to a character
80% — the audience can almost reconstruct the truth
100% — reveal
```

Useful techniques:

- partially covered photographs
- distant figures
- reflections
- shadows
- blurred images
- objects hidden under documents
- cropped bodies
- unreadable screens
- silhouettes
- damaged archival photos

---

## 9. POV Is a Storytelling Tool

First-person perspective is especially useful for:

- exploration
- discovery
- horror
- mystery
- object inspection
- phone screens
- opening boxes
- entering buildings
- walking through corridors
- riding vehicles
- reading documents

Its biggest advantage:

> The audience knows roughly what the protagonist knows.

Third-person shots are useful when the story needs to show:

- the protagonist's appearance
- a large environment
- a vehicle arriving
- historical flashbacks
- relationships between characters
- architecture
- large-scale events

A simple rule:

> **First person is for experience.**

> **Third person is for explanation.**

---

## 10. Avoid the “AI Look”

AI often tries to make everything visually impressive.

That can hurt this storytelling style.

Real phone photos are imperfect.

They may contain:

- awkward framing
- slightly tilted horizons
- messy rooms
- uneven lighting
- motion blur
- noise
- ordinary clothing
- clutter
- imperfect composition
- bad weather
- partially blocked objects

These imperfections create credibility.

Avoid making every shot look like:

- concept art
- a movie poster
- cinematic key art
- luxury advertising
- a perfectly staged photoshoot

The image should feel like:

> **This world already existed. Someone simply happened to photograph it.**

---

## 11. Build a Visual Bible

Long AI stories easily lose continuity.

Before generating many images, define the important visual rules.

At minimum, lock:

- Character appearance and clothing
- Location architecture and layout
- Important props
- Time period and technology level
- Recurring symbols
- Supernatural or science-fiction rules

Once something has appeared, do not redesign it every time.

Think:

> We are returning to an existing place and taking another photo.

---

## 12. Plan Before Generating

Before generating each image, answer five questions:

1. Why does this image exist?
2. What does the protagonist currently know?
3. What is the visual focus?
4. What question should the audience have afterward?
5. What changed from the previous shot?

If nothing changed, the shot may be unnecessary.

---

## 13. Use Minimal Corrections

When an AI-generated image contains one mistake, fix that mistake.

Do not redesign the entire scene.

Examples:

```text
Wrong aspect ratio
→ Fix the aspect ratio
```

```text
Wrong entrance
→ Fix the entrance
```

```text
Extra sign
→ Remove the sign
```

```text
Wrong clothing
→ Restore the correct clothing
```

Every unnecessary change creates new continuity problems.

---

## 14. Design the Reveal Properly

A strong reveal should not simply surprise the audience.

It should change the meaning of earlier scenes.

A useful test is:

> **After learning the truth, would the audience want to go back and rewatch earlier scenes?**

If yes, the reveal is probably doing its job.

---

## 15. Do Not Explain Everything

The main mystery should usually receive an answer.

The entire universe does not have to.

A strong ending can:

```text
Solve one mystery
+
Open a larger mystery
```

This allows individual stories to slowly form a shared universe.

---

## Quick Start

If this is your first AI visual story, use this simplified process.

```text
STEP 1
Write one strange idea.

STEP 2
Write the final truth.

STEP 3
List 5–10 pieces of evidence.

STEP 4
Create 3–5 ordinary-life scenes.

STEP 5
Create 5–7 levels of abnormal escalation.

STEP 6
Define characters, locations, and important props.

STEP 7
Break the story into individual images.

STEP 8
Give each image one main narrative function.

STEP 9
Choose first-person or third-person perspective.

STEP 10
Generate images with continuity in mind.

STEP 11
Remove repetitive shots.

STEP 12
Reveal the main truth.

STEP 13
Leave one final unanswered question.
```

## What This Project Is Not

This project is not:

- a collection of “magic prompts”
- a list of random AI image styles
- a shortcut for generating viral content
- a replacement for storytelling fundamentals
- a guarantee that AI-generated visuals will produce a good story

The framework focuses on:

- narrative design
- visual storytelling
- suspense design
- information control
- first-person storytelling
- AI image direction
- continuity management
- short-form serialized storytelling

## Final Thought

AI can generate characters, buildings, monsters, photographs, laboratories, cities, memories, and entire worlds.

But AI does not decide which image should appear first, which clue should stay hidden, when the audience should understand something, or why the final image should matter.

That is storytelling.

> **AI is the camera.  
> You are the director.**
